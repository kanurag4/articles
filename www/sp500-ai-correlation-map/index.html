How AI-Heavy Is the S&P 500? We Mapped All 500 Companies

KashVector  ·  3 November 2026  ·  12 min read

A note on authorship: The research, analysis, and opinions in this article are the author's own. Claude (Anthropic's AI) assisted with drafting and editing the prose.

We've been listening to a lot of experts lately, and they keep saying the same thing: the S&P 500 is now basically one big AI bet. Buy the index, they say, and you're really buying Nvidia and friends.

And the argument usually goes further than the tech companies. AI isn't only about software and chips. It also runs on the industrial companies building the power and cooling equipment for data centres, and on the utilities selling those data centres their electricity. Add all of that up and, so the story goes, something like 50 to 60% of the index is really riding on AI now.

It sounds right. It also sounds like the kind of thing everyone repeats without checking. So we decided to dig deeper and measure the S&P 500's real AI exposure, company by company, for all 500. How much of the index is really AI stocks, and is that 50–60% true or false?

What's in here:

  • How we measured "AI-ness": two AI funds, and why we blended them into one score
  • Three charts: every company on one line, every sector side by side, and where the money sits
  • Where the 50–60% figure comes from, and how much of it actually moves with AI
  • The answer, which depends on whether you count companies or dollars
  • Where our own undervalued screen sits on the same map

Step 1: two ways to measure "AI"

You can't measure how AI-linked a company is by reading its annual report. Everyone says "AI" now. So we looked at something harder to fake: how its share price actually moved over the last year, compared with the share prices of AI companies.

On KashVector we already show two of these readings for every stock:

  • AI sector correlation: how closely the stock moves with AIQ, a fund holding a broad mix of AI companies (software, internet platforms and chipmakers).
  • AI hardware correlation: how closely it moves with SMH, a fund holding only semiconductor companies, the people who make the chips AI runs on.

There's one important trick. On a day the whole market jumps, almost every stock goes up, AI or not. That tells you nothing about AI. So before comparing, we strip out the part of each day's move that came from the overall market (measured with RSP, a fund that holds all 500 S&P companies in equal amounts). What's left is the part of the move that's specific to that company, and that's what gets compared with AI.

The result is a number between −1 and +1:

  • Close to +1: when AI stocks have a good day beyond the market, this company does too.
  • Around 0: no real link.
  • Negative: on AI's good days, this company tends to lag.

We group the numbers into bands: High (0.40 and up), Moderate (0.20 to 0.40), Low (0.05 to 0.20), None, and Inverse (−0.20 or below). For this article we're calling a company "AI-heavy" if it scores 0.20 or more, so Moderate or High.

Step 2: blending the two into one score

Two readings per company is great on a stock page. For a map of 500 companies, we needed one number. The obvious move is to average the two, but averaging correlations isn't really sound maths. So we built a 50/50 blend: each day, half of AIQ's move plus half of SMH's move. Then we measured every stock against that blend, the same way as before.

We wanted to know whether the blend was quietly changing the answer, so we checked. For 443 of the 498 companies the blended score landed between the two separate readings, and it was never more than 0.05 away from their simple average. It mostly behaves like a fair middle.

Where it does matter is companies that move with one fund but not the other. Microsoft is the best example: 0.31 against the broad AI fund, only 0.07 against chips, and 0.17 on the blend, which puts it just under our AI-heavy line. Hold that thought, because it comes back later.

A word on all those negative scores, because we scratched our heads at this too. Remember we removed the market's average move first? That average includes the AI companies themselves. So on a day AI stocks beat the average, the rest of the index has to trail it, almost by definition. A negative score here means "this company lagged on AI's strong days", usually because money moved out of it and into AI. It doesn't mean it falls when AI falls. That's why we lean on the bands rather than just counting who's left or right of zero.

A few things stood out:

  • Technology is the only sector that really leans AI, with 58% of its companies AI-heavy. But "tech" hides two very different groups. Every chipmaker and chip-equipment company in the index is AI-heavy (median scores of 0.61 and 0.76), and so is most computer hardware. Application software sits at −0.27 and IT services at −0.35. The software companies have been moving against the AI trade this year, not with it.
  • Industrials came second, and not because of robots. The AI-heavy industrials are mostly the companies that build the power behind data centres: Eaton, Vertiv, Caterpillar, GE Vernova, Quanta Services, Comfort Systems. AI needs electricity, and the market has noticed.
  • Healthcare and consumer staples had zero AI-heavy companies. Not one.
  • Consumer Cyclical and Communication Services look tiny by count but big by value. Only 4% of consumer cyclical companies are AI-heavy, but those few (Amazon and Tesla) hold 61% of the sector's value. Same story with Alphabet in communication services.

This is where the experts get some of their point back. The 92 AI-heavy companies are only 18.5% of the companies, but they hold about 37% of the index's value. In dollars, the whole S&P 500 was worth about $68 trillion on 3 November 2026, and $25 trillion of that sat in AI-heavy companies.

And it's concentrated. Nvidia alone is over 7% of the index. Just five companies, Nvidia, Amazon, Alphabet, Broadcom and Tesla, account for $14 trillion, more than half of all the AI-heavy value. Those are the big bubbles on the right.

But notice what sits in the middle: Apple (−0.04), the second-biggest company in the index, has no real link to AI on this measure. And Microsoft at 0.17 is just short of the line. Move the line, or the definition, and the answer moves a lot:

By number of companies, the answer barely changes: somewhere between 17% and 19% whichever way you measure it. By value it swings from about 31% to 44%, and most of that swing is Microsoft deciding which side of the line it's on.

How much of the S&P 500 is AI stocks? Where "50–60%" comes from

We think we found it. Take the sectors people usually put in the AI story (technology, communication services, industrials and utilities) and add up their size. They really are about 55% of the S&P 500's value. That's the number you hear.

The catch is that being in one of those sectors isn't the same as moving with AI. Here's what each one actually contributes once you only count the companies that do:

About half of that 55% genuinely moves with AI. The rest is just sitting in the same sector. The utilities story surprised us most: only 3 of 31 utilities are AI-heavy (Vistra, Constellation Energy and NRG, the power generators selling to data centres), and together they're 0.2% of the index. The other 28 utilities move nothing like AI stocks. Industrials are similar: the data-centre power names are very AI-heavy, but they're a third of the sector's value, not all of it.

The rest of the 37% comes from outside those four sectors, mostly Amazon and Tesla, which sit in consumer cyclical.

So, is it 50–60%? False.

Here's where we landed:

  • The typical company? No. Four out of five S&P 500 companies don't move with AI stocks in any meaningful way.
  • The typical dollar in an index fund? About a third of it (roughly $25 trillion of the index's $68 trillion) sits in companies that move closely with AI.
  • AI exposure is narrow. It's chips, hardware, the power supply chain and a handful of giant platforms. It isn't spread across the economy.

37% of the index linked to AI is high. That's a lot of eggs in one basket, and five companies hold more than half of them. But it isn't the 50–60% we keep hearing in the media. That figure comes from counting whole sectors, and a sector label isn't the same as moving with AI. Even measured only against the broad AI fund, the most generous of our three definitions, it comes to 44%.

How we did it

  • Companies: the current S&P 500 list (502 listings). 498 had enough price history for a score. Four didn't: SATS, EQR, PSKY, VYLR.
  • Period: daily closing prices, adjusted for dividends and splits, from 2 November 2025 to 2 November 2026.
  • Score: the correlation between each stock and a daily 50/50 blend of AIQ and SMH, after removing what the equal-weight S&P 500 (RSP) explains. It's the same calculation behind the AI cards on our stock evaluation page, just with the two funds blended.
  • Size: each company's market value on 3 November 2026. The real index weights companies by the shares available to trade, so the "share of index value" figures here are close to the real index weights but not exact.

This describes how prices moved together over one past year. It's not a forecast, it says nothing about whether any company is a good or bad investment, and it can change quickly when the market's mood changes.

Frequently asked questions

How much of the S&P 500 is AI-heavy?

By number of companies, not much: 92 of the 498 companies we could measure (18.5%) moved closely with AI stocks over the year to 2 November 2026. By size it is a different story: those 92 companies make up about 37% of the S&P 500's total market value (roughly $25 trillion of about $68 trillion), because they include Nvidia, Amazon, Alphabet, Broadcom and Tesla.

Is 50 to 60% of the S&P 500 linked to AI?

Not on this measure. Technology, communication services, industrials and utilities together are about 55% of the index's value, which is likely where the 50 to 60% figure comes from. But only about half of that, 28.6% of the index, is in companies whose shares actually move with AI stocks. Counting every sector, about 37% of the index's value is AI-linked: high, but well short of 50 to 60%.

Which S&P 500 stocks are most linked to AI?

Over the year to 2 November 2026, the S&P 500 stocks most linked to AI were chip-equipment makers and chipmakers: Lam Research (0.83), Micron, AMD, Applied Materials, KLA, Teradyne, Intel, Monolithic Power Systems, Broadcom (0.70) and Nvidia (0.67). Outside chips, the most AI-linked were power and electrical-equipment companies that supply data centres, such as Eaton, Vertiv and Caterpillar. The scores describe how share prices moved in the past, not how they will move.

What is AI sector correlation?

It measures how closely a stock's daily price moves line up with a basket of AI stocks, after removing the part of every move that simply comes from the whole market going up or down. A score near 1 means the stock tends to rise and fall with AI stocks; near 0 means no real link. It describes the past year only and is not a prediction.

Why combine AIQ and SMH into one score?

AIQ is a broad AI fund (software, internet and chips) and SMH holds only chipmakers. Some companies move with one and not the other. Measuring each stock against a 50/50 mix of the two gives one score that counts both kinds of AI exposure, without letting either fund decide on its own.

Which sectors of the S&P 500 are most linked to AI?

Technology by a long way, and within it chipmakers, chip-equipment makers and computer hardware. Next are Industrials, mostly the power and electrical-equipment companies that supply data centres. Healthcare and consumer staples had no AI-heavy companies at all.

What does a negative AI correlation mean here?

Because the score removes the average move of the whole market, a negative number means the company tended to lag on the days AI stocks did well, as money moved elsewhere. It does not mean the company falls whenever AI falls.
