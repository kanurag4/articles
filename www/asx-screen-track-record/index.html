How the screen works

The screen runs a DCF valuation on every ASX 200 stock each week using live financial data from Yahoo Finance. It models free cash flow over a multi-year forecast window, discounts it at a WACC derived from beta, the risk-free rate, and an equity risk premium, and adds a terminal value. Stocks trading at a material discount to that base-case value appear on the list.

When a stock rises to or above its model value — or falls off the list because fresh data narrows the gap — it exits. The exit prices recorded here are the prices at the weekly refresh closest to that moment, not real-time fills. No brokerage, no tax.

Why every gold miner went positive

Pattern: Gold at conservative DCF thresholds

The screen flagged five gold miners between June and September: VAU, NEM, GMD, WGX, and WAF. All five exited positive — returns ranging from +3.7% (West African Resources) to +32.7% (Vault Minerals). None were negative.

Why? The DCF model values gold miners on their trailing free cash flow, which embeds a conservative gold price assumption (the average price over the historical period). During mid-2026, gold equities were pricing a gold pullback that never arrived. Spot gold continued rising, FCF beat expectations, and the model's implied "discount" turned out to be real margin of safety — not modelling error.

The lesson isn't "always buy gold miners when the screen flags them." It's that DCF works best when the conservatism in trailing-history assumptions happens to be conservative in the right direction relative to where spot prices go. Next time the screen flags gold miners at a different point in the gold cycle, the outcome could be different.

Healthcare: two different recovery stories

Ansell (ANN.AX, +16.8% over 53 days) and Ramsay Health Care (RHC.AX, +16.2% in just 13 days) were both flagged while the market was pricing in structural headwinds that the model treated as temporary below-trend FCF. Three healthcare entries, three positive exits — though a 13-day Ramsay exit mostly reflects mean reversion, not a thesis playing out fully.

The iron ore problem with DCF

What DCF can't see: commodity prices

The model flagged Fortescue (FMG.AX) in June because its trailing FCF — at then-prevailing iron ore prices — implied a discount to intrinsic value. What the model had no way to know was that iron ore spot prices were about to fall sharply, driven by slower Chinese steel demand. Fortescue exited at −20.5% over 81 days.

This is the known weak point of trailing-FCF DCF applied to commodity producers: the model treats recent FCF as representative of normal earnings. It isn't. For a miner, the key variable is the commodity price — and that's an external input the model doesn't forecast.

The same logic applies to Beach Energy (oil & gas) and Worley (engineering services heavily exposed to resources capex cycles). When the commodity cycle turns, these names can spend a long time below their "intrinsic value" because the intrinsic value itself is being repriced by the market.

The screen now applies cyclical-normalisation for mining and oil & gas tickers (averaging FCF margin over multiple years rather than using the latest year), which partially addresses this — but it can't eliminate commodity-price risk.

The broader pattern: structural value vs. cycle value

When DCF screening tends to work — and when it doesn't

Works well: Companies with relatively stable FCF that the market has temporarily de-rated due to sentiment, one-off costs, or sector rotation. Healthcare, consumer staples, diversified financials. The model's conservatism often matches (or understates) the actual recoverable earnings power.

Struggles: Companies whose FCF is fundamentally tied to a volatile external price (iron ore, oil, gas, coal). The model computes a "value" that implicitly assumes commodity prices stay near their historical average. When they don't, the margin of safety is illusory.

Gold is interesting: It's technically the same problem — but gold has exhibited a consistent upward trend in AUD terms over this period, so the model's conservatism happened to point in the right direction. Past gold-miner performance here shouldn't be extrapolated as a general rule.

What this means for long-run DCF screening

Four months is not a long enough sample to draw conclusions about whether DCF screening produces above-index returns over the long run — the academic literature on value investing uses 10+ year horizons. What this data does show is the shape of the strategy's outcomes: a slight majority of exits are positive, but the distribution is wide. A −20% loss on FMG and a +32% gain on VAU can coexist in the same portfolio.

The core question value investors ask is whether the average winner exceeds the average loser by enough to cover the losses and still beat a passive alternative. Over this window: the mean positive exit was +8.8% and the mean negative exit was −6.4%. That asymmetry is the right direction — but short holding periods inflate both numbers relative to what a real investor would achieve with real-world transaction costs and tax.

The more practical takeaway is qualitative: the screen has identifiable failure modes (commodity-cycle exposure, short-term re-flags of stocks that already failed), and identifiable strengths (structural FCF recovery, dividend normalisation in insurance). That's useful even if you don't act on the screen's output directly.

Frequently asked questions

Does DCF screening produce profitable results over time?

Across 65 closed positions on the ASX screen (June–September 2026), 42 were positive and 23 were negative — a 64.6% positive-exit rate. The model identifies stocks where current prices imply a lower long-run free cash flow than history and analyst consensus suggest is likely. It does not predict short-term price movements. These are purely mechanical DCF outputs, not financial advice.

Why did DCF miss so badly on Fortescue (FMG.AX)?

The model ingested Fortescue's historical free cash flow and found the stock priced for a worse scenario than those numbers implied. What the model cannot see is an external input — the iron ore spot price — which fell sharply in mid-2026 driven by slower Chinese steel demand. Commodity miners are a known weak spot for trailing-FCF DCF: their value depends on a price the model doesn't forecast.

Why did every gold stock the screen flagged go on to rise?

The screen flagged five gold miners during a period when gold equities were pricing a pullback that never materialised. The DCF model valued them on a conservative trailing-FCF assumption while spot gold kept rising. All five exited positive. This is a case where the model's conservatism happened to point in the right direction — not a general rule about gold miners.

How does the screen decide when to exit a position?

The screen exits when the stock price reaches or exceeds the model's base-case intrinsic value — meaning the margin of safety has closed — or when fresh weekly data no longer shows a significant discount. The screen is refreshed weekly with live data from Yahoo Finance via a Cloudflare Worker.

Is 64% a realistic win rate for a mechanical stock screen?

It is consistent with published academic results on systematic value investing, where positive-exit rates typically fall in the 55–70% range depending on market and holding period. The more important figure is whether average winners exceed average losers — here, the mean positive exit (+8.8%) exceeded the mean negative exit (−6.4%). That said, 65 exits over four months is too short a sample to draw long-run conclusions.
