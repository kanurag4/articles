Intuit (INTU) Valuation Case Study

KashVector  ·  10 October 2026  ·  7 min read

A note on authorship: The research, analysis, and opinions in this article are the author's own. Claude (Anthropic's AI) assisted with drafting and editing the prose.

Every number below is mechanical output — the KashVector DCF tool's free cash flow, discount rate, and growth inputs run through a fixed formula, not an opinion on Intuit's products, competitive position, or management. This is a single-company case study built around a sensitivity table: instead of showing one verdict, it shows how that verdict moves as the two assumptions that matter most — growth and the discount rate — are dialled up and down, and it separates out one thing the formula does mechanically that isn't obvious from the headline number. All figures are as of 3 October 2026, at a price of $281.08, unless stated otherwise. It is not a recommendation on the stock.

The short version:

  • The tool's own Base case: $537.14 intrinsic value vs a $281.08 price, a +91.1% margin of safety (Undervalued) — and Bear ($358.42, +27.5%) and Bull ($820.39, +191.9%) are both Undervalued too.
  • Unlike KashVector's Nike case study, this isn't a story about collapsing cash flow — Intuit's free cash flow base ($6.35B trailing twelve months) is healthy, and the DCF's own growth input (12.4% a year) is derived from real historical growth, not an optimistic guess.
  • Part of the story is mechanical: Intuit's discount rate (9.07%) is unusually low for the sector, and a falling share price is part of why — WACC weights the cost of equity and the cost of debt by market value, so a cheaper equity side mechanically pulls more weight onto the (lower) cost of debt.
  • Stress-testing the discount rate alone, holding growth fixed, the verdict doesn't flip to Overvalued until WACC nearly doubles to ~17% — a wide margin.
  • What the formula can't see: Intuit's own fiscal 2027 guidance (25 August 2026) calls for 9-10% revenue growth, decelerating from 14% the year before, alongside a 17% workforce reduction tied to an AI-strategy pivot — guidance the backward-looking growth input has no way to read.

The mechanical piece: how a falling price can lower its own discount rate

This is the part that's easy to miss. Intuit's WACC (9.07%) blends a cost of equity (9.59%, from a beta of 0.98) and a cost of debt, weighted 90% equity / 10% debt. That 90/10 split isn't fixed — it's recalculated from market value, meaning the "equity" weight is literally today's market capitalisation, not a book-value figure that stays put when the share price moves.

Intuit's share price has fallen a long way: the ticker page's own two-year chart shows it near $610-667 through most of 2025, sliding to roughly $261 by mid-2026, before recovering to today's $281-303 range. As that happened, assuming total debt stayed roughly flat, the equity side of the WACC weighting shrank and the (cheaper) debt side grew as a share of the total. Since Intuit's after-tax cost of debt (~4.4%, implied by the numbers above) sits well below its cost of equity (9.59%), that shift pulls the blended discount rate down — mechanically, with no change in the company's actual business risk required.

Here's why that's worth noticing in plain terms: between late September and early October 2026, Intuit's discount rate actually fell — from about 9.79% down to 9.07%, a drop of roughly 0.72 percentage points. Over that same stretch, every other company on KashVector's S&P 500 undervalued screen moved the opposite way, with discount rates rising by about 0.25 points on average. Intuit going against the grain like that is a big part of why its margin of safety looked so wide. A discount-rate move this size is worth roughly 10% of the final valuation on its own — not a rounding error, as the sensitivity table below shows.

The relationship also runs in reverse, which is a useful way to check it's real rather than a coincidence: a week later, on 9 October, Intuit's price had recovered to $302.75. As the price rose, the discount rate ticked back up to 9.2%, and the margin of safety narrowed from +91.1% to +73.8%. Same mechanism, moving the other direction, playing out live.

The margin-of-safety math here isn't fragile to the discount rate the way it was in Nike's case.

What the numbers can't see

The DCF's growth input is backward-looking by construction — derived from Intuit's own trailing revenue and free-cash-flow history, capped at 15% a year — with no mechanism to read management's forward guidance, news, or market sentiment. Three things from 2026 sit outside that window entirely:

Deceleration in the company's own guidance. Intuit reported fiscal Q4 2026 results on 25 August 2026: revenue up 14% to $4.4B for the quarter and $21.4B for the full year, beating analyst estimates, with adjusted EPS of $4.03 also ahead of estimates. Despite the beat, the stock reportedly slid on the report. The more relevant number for a forward-looking investor is the fiscal 2027 guidance issued the same day: total revenue of roughly $23.3-23.5B, implying 9-10% growth — a clear step down from fiscal 2026's 14%.

A large workforce reduction tied to an AI strategy shift. Earlier in 2026 (announced 20 May), Intuit said it would cut approximately 17% of its workforce — nearly 3,000 roles — with restructuring charges of $300-340M landing in the Q4 results above. The company has framed this as a pivot toward an "AI-native platform" strategy, including a partnership with Anthropic announced in February 2026 to bring AI agents into its products, and used its 17 September 2026 Investor Day to reaffirm the fiscal 2027 guidance under that framing.

A disruption narrative the market is pricing, that the DCF doesn't. Coverage through 2026 has tied part of Intuit's share-price decline to a broader concern that AI-powered tools — automated tax filing, AI financial assistants — could erode the market position of products like TurboTax over time. Whether that concern is well-founded is not something a historical-cash-flow DCF has any way to evaluate; it only sees the cash flow Intuit has already generated, not the risk that a competitive product category gets disrupted from here.

None of this means the DCF's own math is wrong on its own terms — the sensitivity tables above show that math is fairly robust to the two inputs it uses. It means the formula is silent on exactly the things an investor evaluating Intuit today would most want to weigh: a deliberate deceleration in management's own guidance, a large restructuring, and a live disruption debate. Frequently asked questions

Is this article a recommendation to buy or avoid Intuit stock?

No. Every figure here is mechanical output from running Intuit's own reported cash flows through a Discounted Cash Flow formula with stated assumptions — it is not a view on Intuit's products, competitive position, or management, and it is not a signal to act on the stock.

Why does the DCF say Intuit is undervalued by over 90%?

Two things, separate from the share price. First, Intuit's free cash flow base ($6.35B trailing twelve months) is healthy and still growing — nothing like Nike's shrinking cash flow in KashVector's other case study. Second, Intuit's discount rate (WACC) is unusually low at 9.07% for a software company, and part of that is mechanical: WACC blends the cost of equity and the cost of debt weighted by market value, so when a falling share price shrinks the equity side of that weighting, more of the blend shifts toward the cheaper cost of debt — pulling the discount rate down even if nothing about the underlying business risk changed.

How sensitive is Intuit's valuation to the discount rate?

Less than the headline number suggests. Holding growth at the Base case's 12.4%, the discount rate would need to rise from 9.07% to roughly 17% — nearly double — before the verdict flips from Undervalued to Overvalued. That is a wide margin by the standard of most DCF sensitivity tests, and one reason it's wide is that Intuit's cash flow (the other half of the formula) isn't under the same pressure Nike's is.

What does the DCF model not account for?

The model's growth input is backward-looking — derived from Intuit's own trailing revenue and free-cash-flow history, capped at 15% a year — with no mechanism to read forward guidance. Intuit's own fiscal 2027 guidance, issued 25 August 2026, calls for 9-10% revenue growth, down from fiscal 2026's 14%, and the company cut 17% of its workforce the same year citing an AI-driven strategy shift. None of that guidance, the workforce cut, or the market's own AI-disruption concerns about tax software are inputs the DCF formula uses.

Is Intuit (INTU) undervalued right now?

Per the tool's own Base case as of 3 October 2026, yes on the formula's own terms — $537.14 intrinsic value against a $281.08 price, a +91.1% margin of safety (Undervalued).
