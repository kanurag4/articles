Why Would a Company Pay You to Own It? Dividends and Franking Credits for Beginners

This is Part 5 of our Stock Market Basics series. In Part 4 we met BeanRush, a young coffee business spending every dollar it has, and more, to open new cafés. This time we go to the other end of a company's life: a business that makes more cash than it needs, and starts handing some of it to its owners.

That payment is called a dividend. In Australia it often comes with something extra, called a franking credit, which can cut your tax bill or even put cash back in your pocket. It's a big reason Australians love dividend-paying shares.

  • What a dividend is, plus dividend yield and payout ratio
  • The dividend discount model: a third way to value our coffee shop
  • Franking credits: how they work, and why retirees and super funds like them so much

Step 1: What is a dividend?

Here's our coffee shop again, as we left it in Part 3:

Profit (earnings) each year$20,000
Free cash flow each year$15,000
Cash in the bank$40,000
Bank loan$30,000
Number of shares100
Price offered per share$2,000

Say the landlord rents the empty space next door to someone else, so there's no second counter to build. The shop still produces $15,000 of free cash flow every year, the cash left after paying the running costs and replacing worn-out equipment. It doesn't need that money for anything. So the owners decide to pay it out to themselves.

A company can't pay one owner more than another, so the money is split equally across the shares: $15,000 ÷ 100 shares = $150 per share. An owner with 20 shares receives $3,000.

A dividend = a share of the company's cash, paid to its owners, per share.

Australian companies usually pay twice a year; US companies usually pay four times a year.

Two numbers help you compare dividends between companies:

  • Dividend yield = dividend per share ÷ share price. At the $2,000 offer price: $150 ÷ $2,000 = 7.5%. It's the dividend you get back each year for every dollar you pay, a bit like the interest rate on a bank account.
  • Payout ratio = dividend ÷ profit. The shop earns $200 profit per share (from Part 2) and pays $150 of it out: a payout ratio of 75%. The other 25% is the money it spends replacing equipment.

One warning about yield: it moves with the share price. If the price falls to $1,500, the same $150 dividend is a 10% yield. A very high yield can simply mean investors expect the dividend to be cut.

Step 2: Why some companies pay, and others don't

BeanRush from Part 4 pays no dividend at all. It burns cash, and every dollar it has goes into new cafés. Paying a dividend would mean borrowing or selling new shares just to hand the money back, which makes no sense.

That's the general pattern. A company with good ways to grow usually keeps its cash and reinvests it. A company that has more cash than good ideas pays it out. Paying a dividend isn't a sign a company is better or worse; it tells you where it is in its life.

There's a second way to hand cash back, called a share buyback: the company buys some of its own shares on the stock market and cancels them, so each remaining share owns a bigger slice. You saw this in Part 4, where Toyota's share count fell about 4% since 2022. Many US companies prefer buybacks to dividends. Australian companies lean towards dividends, for a tax reason we'll get to in Step 4.

Step 3: Valuing the shop from its dividends

In Part 3 we valued the shop by adding up all its future free cash flow, discounted back to today. A dividend discount model, or DDM, does the same thing with a narrower question: how much cash will actually reach me, the owner?

Dividend discount model (DDM) = a share is worth all the dividends it will pay in future, with each one discounted back to today.

The shop pays $150 a share every year, forever. Using the same 10% discount rate and the same "divide by the rate" shortcut as the terminal value in Part 3:

$150 ÷ 0.10 = $1,500 per share

Part 3's DCF gave $1,600. Why $100 less? Look at the bank account. The shop has $40,000 of cash and a $30,000 loan: $10,000 more cash than debt, or $100 a share. The DCF counted that spare cash as the owners'. The DDM doesn't, because it isn't being paid out: it just sits in the shop's account. If the shop paid the spare $10,000 out as a one-off extra dividend, owners would get $100 a share today plus $150 a year after that, and the DDM would come to $1,600 too.

That's the key idea: when a company pays out all the cash it can spare, the dividend model and the cash flow model give the same answer. They're two views of the same money.

What if the dividend grows?

Suppose the shop can lift its prices a little each year, so the dividend grows 2% a year. The shortcut changes the same way it did in Part 3: take next year's dividend and divide by the discount rate minus the growth rate.

Value = next year's dividend ÷ (discount rate − growth rate)
$153 ÷ (0.10 − 0.02) = $1,912.50

So the stranger's $2,000 offer makes sense if you believe the dividend will grow by about 2.3% a year, forever. That's the same question every part of this series has ended on: what would you have to believe? Notice also how sensitive the answer is. One extra percentage point of growth, from 2% to 3%, adds almost $300 a share.

The DDM has one big weakness: it trusts the dividend. It works well for companies that pay steady, predictable dividends year after year. For a company whose dividend jumps up and down, or that pays none at all like BeanRush, it gives a misleading answer, and a cash flow model is the better tool. We'll see a real example of both at the end.

Step 4: Franking credits, and why Australians love dividends

Here's a problem with dividends. A company pays tax on its profit: in Australia, 30% for most large companies. Then it pays a dividend out of what's left, and the owner pays income tax on that dividend too. The same profit gets taxed twice.

Since 1987, Australia has fixed this with a system called dividend imputation. When an Australian company pays a dividend out of profit it has already paid tax on, it attaches a franking credit: a note saying "we've already paid this much tax on your behalf".

Franking credit = the company tax already paid on the profit behind your dividend. You get credit for it on your own tax return.

A fully franked dividend carries credits for the full 30%. Partly franked carries some; unfranked carries none.

Let's follow $100 of company profit:

  1. The company pays $30 tax and pays the remaining $70 to you as a fully franked dividend, with a $30 franking credit.
  2. On your tax return you declare the cash plus the credit: $70 + $30 = $100. This is called the grossed-up dividend.
  3. You work out tax on $100 at your own rate, then subtract the $30 the company already paid.

Here's what that looks like at Australia's 2026–27 tax rates (including the 2% Medicare levy), next to a $70 dividend with no franking:

Two things stand out:

  • The profit is only ever taxed once, at your rate. Whatever your rate, you end up with $100 of profit minus your tax, just as if you'd earned it yourself. The company tax is a down payment, not an extra cost.
  • If your rate is below 30%, the difference comes back to you as cash. Since 2000, unused franking credits have been refunded. A retiree paying no tax gets the full $30 back: $100 in their pocket from a $70 dividend.

That's why so many Australian retirees and self-managed super funds hold fully franked shares like Commonwealth Bank, Telstra and BHP. For them, a 4% fully franked yield is really worth about 5.7% (4% ÷ 0.7), the grossed-up yield. It's also why Australian companies tend to pay dividends rather than buy back shares: a buyback doesn't pass franking credits to most owners.

A few details worth knowing:

  • Only Australian tax counts. Franking credits come from tax paid to the Australian Tax Office. Dividends from overseas companies are always unfranked, and a company that earns most of its profit overseas may pay only partly franked dividends.
  • Smaller companies frank at 25%. Companies with turnover under $50 million pay 25% company tax, so their credits are smaller (a $75 dividend with a $25 credit). The idea is the same.
  • The 45-day rule. To claim more than $5,000 of franking credits in a year, you generally have to hold the shares for at least 45 days around the dividend. It stops people buying shares just before a dividend to collect the credits.

Do ASX companies pay bigger dividends than the S&P 500?

The answer surprised us. More American companies pay a dividend, but most pay a small one: nearly half the S&P 500 yields under 2%, because many US companies hand cash back through buybacks instead. On the ASX, a typical payer yields almost twice as much, and nearly a third of the ASX 300 yields 4% or more. Many of those dividends also come with franking credits, which the cash yields in the table don't even count. A fully franked 3.5% is worth about 5% before tax.

That's franking at work. In the US there are no franking credits, so a dividend is taxed again in the owner's hands straight away, while a buyback lets owners put off any tax until they sell. An Australian company that has paid Australian tax can pass the credit on with a dividend, so its owners usually prefer the cash.

A bigger dividend doesn't mean a bigger return.

Remember from Part 1 that shares make money in two ways: dividends and capital gains (the share price going up). This table only measures the first. Cash a company keeps instead of paying out, or spends on buybacks, can still reach its owners through a rising share price. A company with a low yield can give a bigger total return than one with a high yield, and the reverse.

The two are also taxed differently. A dividend is taxed as income in the year you receive it (with franking credits for Australian companies). A capital gain is only taxed when you sell, under the capital gains tax rules, which have their own discounts and changes coming from 1 July 2027 under the 2026 Budget. So compare total returns, after tax, for your own situation, not yields alone.

Coca-Cola is the steady payer. Its dividend has gone up every single year in the table, and it has raised it every year for more than 60 years in a row. People keep buying drinks in good times and bad, so its cash flow is steady, and so is its dividend. That's exactly the kind of company the dividend discount model from Step 3 was made for.

BHP is the swinging payer. Its policy is to pay out at least half of its profit, and its profit rises and falls with the prices of iron ore and copper. When those prices were high in 2021–22, BHP paid more than A$4 a share; by 2025 it was A$1.71. A DDM that assumed 2022's dividend would grow forever would wildly overvalue BHP; one built on 2025's would undervalue it. For a company like this, a cash flow valuation that averages several years of results is the better tool. Our DCF Valuation tool does exactly that for mining companies.

And for an Australian investor, the tax changes the picture. BHP's dividends are fully franked, so its 4.1% cash yield is worth about 5.9% before tax. Coca-Cola's dividend has no franking, and the US keeps 15% of it as tax (if you've filled in a W-8BEN form with your broker; 30% if you haven't). An Australian taxpayer can usually claim that 15% back against their Australian tax as a foreign income tax offset, but a retiree who pays no tax can't get it back. For that retiree, BHP's $100 grossed-up dividend arrives as $100; Coca-Cola's $100 dividend arrives as $85.

None of this makes either company a better or worse investment. A higher yield can come with a less reliable dividend, and a lower yield can come from a company the market expects to keep growing. Each tool fits a different kind of company. That's the real lesson.

Three things to remember

  1. A dividend is cash the company doesn't need, paid to its owners. Mature companies pay them; growing companies usually keep their cash. Yield = dividend ÷ price; payout ratio = dividend ÷ profit.
  2. A dividend discount model values a share from the dividends it will pay. Next year's dividend ÷ (discount rate − growth rate). It suits steady payers like Coca-Cola, not swinging ones like BHP.
  3. Franking credits mean company profit is taxed once, at your rate. If your rate is under 30%, the difference is refunded, which is why retirees and super funds like fully franked Australian shares.

Frequently asked questions

What is a dividend?

A dividend is a share of a company's cash that it pays out to its owners, usually twice a year in Australia and four times a year in the US. It is paid per share: if a company pays a $1 dividend and you own 100 shares, you receive $100. Companies don't have to pay dividends, and they can raise, cut or stop them at any time.

What are franking credits?

Franking credits are a record of the company tax an Australian company has already paid on the profit behind a dividend. When you receive a franked dividend, you count the dividend plus its franking credit as income, work out your tax at your own rate, and then subtract the credit. This stops the same profit being taxed twice: once in the company and again in your hands.

Can you get a refund of franking credits?

Yes. If your tax rate is lower than the 30% company tax rate, the leftover franking credit is refunded to you when you lodge your tax return. Someone on a 0% tax rate, such as many retirees or a super fund in pension phase, gets the whole credit back as cash. That refund is a big reason why Australian retirees and self-managed super funds like fully franked dividends.

What is a grossed-up dividend yield?

The grossed-up yield adds the franking credit to the cash dividend before dividing by the share price. A fully franked dividend at the 30% company tax rate is worth the cash dividend divided by 0.7 before tax, so a 4% fully franked yield is about a 5.7% grossed-up yield. It lets you compare franked dividends fairly with interest or unfranked dividends.

Do US shares like Coca-Cola come with franking credits?

No. Franking credits come only from tax paid to the Australian Tax Office, so dividends from overseas companies are unfranked. US companies also withhold 15% of the dividend for US tax when an Australian investor has lodged a W-8BEN form (30% without one). Australian taxpayers can usually claim that withheld amount as a foreign income tax offset, but it is not refunded to someone who pays no Australian tax.

What is the dividend discount model?

The dividend discount model (DDM) values a share as all the dividends it will pay in future, each discounted back to today. For a dividend that grows at a steady rate forever, the shortcut is next year's dividend divided by (discount rate minus growth rate). It works best for companies that pay steady, predictable dividends, such as banks and consumer staples, and poorly for companies whose dividends swing up and down.
